Put a clear plastic ruler between two polarizing sheets and bend it, and rainbow-coloured fringes appear. Engineers once built scale models of bridges and machine parts this way and counted the fringes to see where the forces concentrate, by eye, with no computer. In this experiment you rebuild that method from the start.

The fringes appear because light travels at different speeds along the direction in which the material is stretched and the direction in which it is compressed. The difference between the two refractive indices is proportional to the difference between the two principal stresses. The pattern of bright and dark fringes is therefore a map of the stress. To count fringes is to measure stress.

::: box Apparatus
- 2 linear polarizing films ($150 \times 150\,\mathrm{mm}$), 2 quarter-wave plate films
- green LED backlight panel (centre wavelength $\lambda = 546\,\mathrm{nm}$), A5 size
- 3 specimens cut from polyurethane photoelastic sheet: tension specimen (width $20.0\,\mathrm{mm}$), bending beam (depth $25.0\,\mathrm{mm}$), tension specimen with a hole (width $30.0\,\mathrm{mm}$, hole diameter $6.0\,\mathrm{mm}$). All specimens are $6.00\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick.
- set of masses ($50\,\mathrm{g}\times4$, $100\,\mathrm{g}\times4$, $500\,\mathrm{g}\times6$), mass hanger
- 2 clamp stands, clip, pulley, thread
- $300\,\mathrm{mm}$ ruler, vernier caliper, protractor, marker pen
:::

::: box Caution
Fix the stands firmly so that the masses cannot fall. Do not fold or scratch the photoelastic specimens. Once a specimen deforms plastically it keeps residual fringes, and every later measurement is spoiled. Do not load the tension specimen with more than $4\,\mathrm{kg}$.
:::

### Background

When two principal stresses $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ act in a specimen of thickness $t$, light polarized along the two principal directions acquires a phase difference, and dark fringes appear in a crossed polariscope. At the $N$-th dark fringe

$$\sigma_1 - \sigma_2 = \frac{N f_\sigma}{t}, \qquad N = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$$

The quantity $f_\sigma$ is called the *stress fringe value*. It depends on the material and on the wavelength. The stress-optic coefficient is defined as $C = \lambda / f_\sigma$.

With only two linear polarizers, dark lines (isoclinics) also appear wherever a principal stress direction is parallel to a polarizer axis, and the fringe order becomes hard to count. If a quarter-wave plate is placed between each polarizer and the specimen with its optic axis at $45^\circ$ to the polarizer axis, the isoclinics disappear and only the isochromatics remain.

In simple tension $\sigma_2 = 0$, so $\sigma_1 = F/A$. In bending, $\sigma_1 - \sigma_2 = 0$ on the neutral axis and is largest at the surface, varying linearly with depth in between. For a beam with second moment of area $I$ under a bending moment $M$, the stress at a distance $y$ from the neutral axis is $\sigma = My/I$.

## Part A - Building the polariscope (1.5 pt)

::: q A.1 0.6pt
Place the two polarizers on top of each other and rotate one of them. Observe how the transmitted brightness changes. Find the angle at which it is darkest and write down the angle between the two transmission axes at that point. This setting is the reference for all later measurements.
:::

::: q A.2 0.9pt
Place the tension specimen in the crossed polariscope. With no load applied, record the fringe order $N_0$ you see, and if there are fringes, state their cause. Then load the specimen with the hanger and $100\,\mathrm{g}$, rotate it in its own plane from $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ and watch. Insert the two quarter-wave plates and repeat. State what differs between the two cases and explain why the quarter-wave plates are needed.
:::

## Part B - Measuring the stress fringe value (2.6 pt)

::: q B.1 1.0pt
In the circular polariscope, load the tension specimen and measure the fringe order $N$ in its uniform part as a function of the load $F$. Vary the load from $0.5\,\mathrm{kg}$ to $3.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ in at least 6 steps. Determine $N$ by counting the fringes that pass across the specimen, and state how you read orders that are not integers.
:::

::: q B.2 1.0pt
Plot $N$ against $F$ and find the slope of the line. From it, find the stress fringe value $f_\sigma$ and the stress-optic coefficient $C$, with uncertainties.
:::

::: q B.3 0.6pt
If the line does not pass through the origin, what does the intercept mean? State which quantity contributes most to the uncertainty of $f_\sigma$, and what you would do, and how, to halve that contribution.
:::

## Part C - A stress map inside a beam (2.9 pt)

::: q C.1 1.0pt
Clamp the bending beam to make a cantilever and hang $100\,\mathrm{g}$ from its free end. Choose a cross-section at a fixed distance from the clamped end and measure the positions of the fringe orders across the depth of that section. Tabulate the distance $y_N$ of each fringe from the neutral axis.
:::

::: q C.2 1.0pt
Plot $N$ against $y_N$ and check whether it is a straight line. Determine the position of the neutral axis from the experiment and compare it with the geometric centre of the beam.
:::

::: q C.3 0.9pt
Find $f_\sigma$ again from the slope in C.2 and the bending moment. Compare it with the value from B.2. If the two differ, decide which one is more reliable and give your reasons.
:::

## Part D - How much does a hole raise the stress? Your own design (3.0 pt)

In this part you must choose the measurement procedure yourself. On the answer sheet, state what you measured, how, and why. Numerical results without a written procedure receive no marks.

::: q D.1 1.2pt
Design and carry out a procedure to determine the stress concentration factor

$$K_t = \frac{(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)_{\text{edge of hole}}}{(\sigma_1 - \sigma_2)_{\text{far from hole}}}$$

of the tension specimen with a hole. State the load you chose, where and how you read the fringe orders, and how you dealt with the difficulty of reading the order at the edge of the hole.
:::

::: q D.2 0.9pt
Find $K_t$ and its uncertainty.
:::

::: q D.3 0.9pt
For a small circular hole in an infinitely wide plate the theoretical value is $K_t = 3.00$. If your value differs, decide with reasons whether the finite width of the specimen tends to make $K_t$ larger or smaller, and name at least one cause of the remaining difference.
:::
