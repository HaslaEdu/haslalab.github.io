## Part A - Why a water clock slows down (1.2 pt)

::: small
Syllabus: fluid mechanics (Bernoulli's equation) and the mathematics appendix (first-order separable differential equations). No further concepts are needed.
:::

::: q A.1 0.5pt
Differential equation of the water clock and total discharge time $T_w$.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

The volume flow rate out of the hole is $a\sqrt{2gh}$, and the water in the cylinder decreases by this amount.

$$A\frac{dh}{dt} = -a\sqrt{2gh}.$$

Separating variables and integrating from $h_0$ to $0$ gives

$$T_w = \frac{A}{a}\int_0^{h_0}\frac{dh}{\sqrt{2gh}} = \frac{A}{a}\sqrt{\frac{2h_0}{g}}.$$

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $A\,dh/dt = -a\sqrt{2gh}$ | 0.3 |
| $T_w = (A/a)\sqrt{2h_0/g}$ | 0.2 |
:::

::: q A.2 0.3pt
Numerical value of $T_w$.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$\frac{a}{A} = \left(\frac{D}{2R}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{2.40}{24.0}\right)^2 = 1.00\times10^{-2},$$

$$T_w = \frac{1}{1.00\times10^{-2}}\sqrt{\frac{2\times0.126}{9.81}} = 16.0\,\mathrm{s}.$$

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $a/A = (D/2R)^2 = 1.00\times10^{-2}$ | 0.1 |
| $T_w = 16.0\,\mathrm{s}$ (15.8–16.2) | 0.2 |
:::

::: q A.3 0.4pt
Ratio of the draining times of the two halves, and the condition for steady time.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

The time to drain from height $h$ to the bottom is $t(h) = (A/a)\sqrt{2h/g} \propto \sqrt{h}$. The first half takes $t(h_0) - t(h_0/2) \propto \sqrt{h_0}\,(1 - 1/\sqrt2)$ and the second half takes $\propto \sqrt{h_0}/\sqrt2$. Hence

$$\frac{t_{\text{first}}}{t_{\text{second}}} = \sqrt2 - 1 = 0.414.$$

The first half takes $29.3\,\%$ of the total time and the second half $70.7\,\%$. A water clock slows down because its flow rate depends on the remaining height. For a clock to keep steady time, the mass flow rate must be constant, independent of the amount left.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| From $t(h)\propto\sqrt h$, first half $\propto (1 - 1/\sqrt2)$ | 0.2 |
| Ratio $= \sqrt2 - 1 = 0.414$ (first $29.3\,\%$ : second $70.7\,\%$) | 0.1 |
| Steady time needs a flow rate independent of the amount left | 0.1 |
:::

## Part B - Janssen stress saturation (3.3 pt)

::: small
Syllabus: statics (friction) and the mathematics appendix (first-order linear differential equations with constant coefficients). The Janssen coefficient $K$ and the wall friction law are outside the syllabus, so the text gives them as formulas [1]. Part B deals with the pile at rest with the outlet closed and has nothing to do with the flow.
:::

::: q B.1 1.1pt
$\sigma_v(z)$ from the force balance on a thin layer.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

The depth $z$ is measured downwards from the free surface. The vertical forces on the layer between $z$ and $z+dz$ are: $\sigma_v(z+dz)\,\pi R^2$ upwards on its lower face, $\sigma_v(z)\,\pi R^2$ downwards on its upper face, its weight $\rho_b g\,\pi R^2 dz$, and the wall friction $\tau_w\,2\pi R\,dz = \mu_w K\sigma_v\,2\pi R\,dz$ acting on the perimeter $2\pi R$. In equilibrium

$$\pi R^2\,d\sigma_v = \rho_b g\,\pi R^2 dz - \mu_w K\sigma_v\,2\pi R\,dz,$$

so

$$\frac{d\sigma_v}{dz} = \rho_b g - \frac{2\mu_w K}{R}\,\sigma_v.$$

This is a first-order linear equation with constant coefficients. Its solution with $\sigma_v(0)=0$ is

$$\sigma_v(z) = \rho_b g\lambda\left(1 - e^{-z/\lambda}\right), \qquad \lambda = \frac{R}{2\mu_w K}.$$

At depths much greater than $\lambda$ the stress saturates at $\rho_b g\lambda$. For a liquid $K=0$, so $\lambda\to\infty$ and the hydrostatic pressure is recovered.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| Wall friction $\mu_w K\sigma_v\,2\pi R\,dz$ included in the force balance on the layer | 0.3 |
| $d\sigma_v/dz = \rho_b g - (2\mu_w K/R)\sigma_v$ | 0.3 |
| Uses $\sigma_v(0)=0$, $\sigma_v = \rho_b g\lambda(1 - e^{-z/\lambda})$ | 0.3 |
| $\lambda = R/(2\mu_w K)$ | 0.2 |
:::

::: q B.2 0.4pt
$\lambda$ and $\sigma_v(h_0)$, compared with hydrostatic pressure.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$\lambda = \frac{R}{2\mu_w K} = \frac{12.0\times10^{-3}}{2\times0.40\times0.50} = 3.00\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{m} = 3.00\,\mathrm{cm},$$

$$\rho_b = \varphi\rho_p = 1.50\times10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}, \qquad \rho_b g\lambda = 441\,\mathrm{Pa}.$$

Since $h_0/\lambda = 4.20$,

$$\sigma_v(h_0) = 441\times\left(1 - e^{-4.20}\right) = 435\,\mathrm{Pa}.$$

The hydrostatic pressure at the same depth would be $\rho_b g h_0 = 1854\,\mathrm{Pa}$. The actual stress at the base is only $1/4.26$ of it.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $\lambda = 3.00\,\mathrm{cm}$ | 0.2 |
| $\sigma_v(h_0) = 435\,\mathrm{Pa}$ (430–440) | 0.1 |
| $1/4.3$ of the hydrostatic $1854\,\mathrm{Pa}$ | 0.1 |
:::

::: q B.3 0.5pt
Ratio of the force on the base to the total weight.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$F = \sigma_v(h_0)\,\pi R^2 = 435\times4.524\times10^{-4} = 0.197\,\mathrm{N},$$

$$M = \rho_b\,\pi R^2 h_0 = 85.5\,\mathrm{g}, \qquad Mg = 0.839\,\mathrm{N}.$$

$$\frac{F}{Mg} = 23.5\,\%.$$

The remaining $76.5\,\%$ is carried by wall friction. This is the Janssen effect: the base under a column of grains carries much less than the weight of the grains.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $F = \sigma_v(h_0)\pi R^2 = 0.197\,\mathrm{N}$ (0.19–0.20) | 0.2 |
| $M = 85.5\,\mathrm{g}$, $Mg = 0.839\,\mathrm{N}$ | 0.1 |
| $23.5\,\%$ (22–25), the rest carried by wall friction | 0.2 |
:::

::: q B.4 0.6pt
Fraction of time for which saturation fails, and the limit of the Janssen explanation.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$1 - e^{-h/\lambda} \geq 0.95$ gives $h \geq \lambda\ln 20 = 3.00\lambda = 9.0\,\mathrm{cm}$. With a constant mass flow rate the remaining height falls linearly in time, so the fraction of the total discharge time with $h < 9.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ is

$$\frac{9.0}{12.6} = 71\,\%.$$

For most of the running time the stress at the base is more than $5\,\%$ below its saturated value, and at the end it drops to $0$. Janssen saturation alone cannot explain the steady running. The cause must be sought in the arch above the outlet, not in the pressure.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $1 - e^{-h/\lambda} \geq 0.95 \Rightarrow h \geq 3\lambda = 9.0\,\mathrm{cm}$ | 0.3 |
| Fraction of time $= 9.0/12.6 = 71\,\%$ | 0.2 |
| Janssen saturation alone does not explain steady time | 0.1 |
:::

::: q B.5 0.7pt
Apparent mass on the balance.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

The balance takes the force carried by the base, so from the solution of B.1

$$M_{\mathrm{app}}(h) = \frac{\sigma_v(h)\,\pi R^2}{g} = \rho_b\pi R^2\lambda\left(1 - e^{-h/\lambda}\right).$$

The bracket never exceeds 1 however large $h$ becomes, so the apparent mass saturates at

$$M_{\mathrm{app}}^{\infty} = \rho_b\pi R^2\lambda = 1.50\times10^{3}\times4.524\times10^{-4}\times3.00\times10^{-2} = 2.04\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{kg} = 20.4\,\mathrm{g}.$$

At $h_0 = 12.6\,\mathrm{cm}$ it is $20.4\times0.985 = 20.1\,\mathrm{g}$. This equals $F/g = 0.197\,\mathrm{N}/g = 20.1\,\mathrm{g}$ from B.3.

Filling this cylinder to a height of $1\,\mathrm{m}$ would still give a reading of $20.4\,\mathrm{g}$, although the true mass is then $679\,\mathrm{g}$. All the extra weight is carried by wall friction and goes into the stand. Janssen observed exactly this in grain silos in 1895. It is also why silo walls are built much stronger than their floors.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $M_{\mathrm{app}} = \rho_b\pi R^2\lambda(1 - e^{-h/\lambda})$ | 0.3 |
| Saturated value $M_{\mathrm{app}}^{\infty} = \rho_b\pi R^2\lambda = 20.4\,\mathrm{g}$ (19–22) | 0.2 |
| $20.1\,\mathrm{g}$ at $h_0$, agrees with B.3 | 0.1 |
| The rest of the weight is carried by wall friction | 0.1 |
:::

## Part C - The free-fall arch and the Beverloo law (3.8 pt)

::: small
Syllabus: mechanics (free fall) and dimensional analysis. The free-fall arch model and the Beverloo constant are outside the syllabus and are given in the text [2, 3]. So is the jamming condition [4].
:::

::: q C.1 1.0pt
The arch model and derivation of $Q_m \propto \rho_b\sqrt g D^{5/2}$.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

Below the arch there is no stress, so the grains start from rest and fall freely. Let the arch height be $H_{\mathrm{arch}} = k_1 D$ (assumption of the text, $k_1$ a dimensionless constant). The speed through the outlet is

$$v = \sqrt{2gH_{\mathrm{arch}}} = \sqrt{2k_1}\,\sqrt{gD} \;\propto\; \sqrt{gD}.$$

The outlet area is $\frac{\pi}{4}D^2$, so the mass flow rate is

$$Q_m = \rho_b\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}D^2\cdot v \;\propto\; \rho_b\sqrt g\,D^{5/2}.$$

Dimensional analysis gives the same result. If $Q_m$ depends only on $\rho_b$, $g$ and $D$, then $Q_m = \mathrm{const}\times\rho_b^{\alpha}g^{\beta}D^{\gamma}$ has the unique solution $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = \tfrac12$, $\gamma = \tfrac52$. The key point is that $h$ does not appear anywhere.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| Zero stress below the arch, free fall from rest | 0.3 |
| Arch height $\propto D \Rightarrow v \propto \sqrt{gD}$ | 0.3 |
| $Q_m = \rho_b(\pi/4)D^2 v \propto \rho_b\sqrt g D^{5/2}$ | 0.4 |
:::

::: q C.2 0.6pt
The empty-ring correction and steady running.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

A grain can pass only if its centre is at least $d/2$ inside the edge of the outlet. The effective diameter of the flow is therefore $D - kd$ rather than $D$, with $k$ a dimensionless constant of order $1$:

$$Q_m = C\rho_b\sqrt g\,(D - kd)^{5/2}.$$

A ring of width $d/2$ on each side gives $k = 1$, but this argument fixes only the order of $k$. The value $k = 1.4$ in Table 1 is measured [2].

The right-hand side contains neither the remaining height $h$ nor the stress at the base. The discharge rate is set by the geometry of the outlet alone and does not depend on how much sand lies above it. Hence $dM/dt = -Q_m$ is constant and the hourglass keeps steady time.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| Empty ring of width $\sim d/2$ at the edge $\Rightarrow$ effective diameter $D - kd$ | 0.3 |
| $Q_m = C\rho_b\sqrt g(D - kd)^{5/2}$ | 0.2 |
| No $h$ in the formula, so the flow rate is constant $\Rightarrow$ steady time | 0.1 |
:::

::: q C.3 0.6pt
Numerical values of $Q_m$, $M$ and $T$.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$D - kd = 2.40 - 1.4\times0.30 = 1.98\,\mathrm{mm},$$

$$Q_m = 0.58\times1.50\times10^{3}\times\sqrt{9.81}\times(1.98\times10^{-3})^{5/2} = 4.75\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}},$$

$$M = \rho_b\,\pi R^2 h_0 = 1.50\times10^{3}\times4.524\times10^{-4}\times0.126 = 8.55\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{kg},$$

$$T = \frac{M}{Q_m} = \frac{8.55\times10^{-2}}{4.75\times10^{-4}} = 180\,\mathrm{s}.$$

That is exactly three minutes. Water in the same cylinder with the same hole took $16.0\,\mathrm{s}$. The sand runs out 11 times more slowly, and its rate stays the same to the end.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $D - kd = 1.98\,\mathrm{mm}$ | 0.1 |
| $Q_m = 4.75\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$ (4.6–4.9) | 0.2 |
| $M = 85.5\,\mathrm{g}$ | 0.1 |
| $T = 180\,\mathrm{s}$ (175–185) | 0.2 |
:::

::: q C.4 0.4pt
Consistency of the outlet speed and the arch height.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$v = \frac{Q_m}{\rho_b\,\frac{\pi}{4}(D - kd)^2} = \frac{4.75\times10^{-4}}{1.50\times10^{3}\times3.079\times10^{-6}} = 0.103\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},$$

$$H_{\mathrm{arch}} = \frac{v^2}{2g} = \frac{(0.103)^2}{2\times9.81} = 5.4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m} = 0.54\,\mathrm{mm} \approx \frac{D}{4}.$$

The arch height is of the same order as the outlet diameter, which is consistent with the assumption that the arch height is proportional to $D$.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $v = Q_m/[\rho_b\pi(D - kd)^2/4] = 0.103\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | 0.2 |
| $H = v^2/2g = 0.54\,\mathrm{mm} \approx D/4$, consistent with the model | 0.2 |
:::

::: q C.5 0.5pt
Jamming limit and design margin.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

From the experimental jamming condition $D/d \gtrsim 6$ [4],

$$D_{\mathrm{min}} \approx 6d = 6\times0.30 = 1.8\,\mathrm{mm}.$$

Table 1 gives $D/d = 2.40/0.30 = 8.0$, so the margin above the limit is only

$$\frac{8.0 - 6}{6} = 33\,\%.$$

Slightly larger grains or a slightly smaller hole, and the arch stays in place and the flow stops. This is why hourglass makers select the grain size carefully.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $D_{\mathrm{min}} \approx 6d = 1.8\,\mathrm{mm}$ | 0.2 |
| $D/d = 8.0$ | 0.2 |
| Margin $33\,\%$ | 0.1 |
:::

::: q C.6 0.7pt
Grains of twice the diameter.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$D - kd = 2.40 - 1.4\times0.60 = 1.56\,\mathrm{mm},$$

$$\frac{T'}{T} = \left(\frac{1.98}{1.56}\right)^{5/2} = 1.81 \;\Longrightarrow\; T' = 1.81\times180 = 327\,\mathrm{s}.$$

Doubling the grain size alone makes the discharge 1.8 times longer. The empty ring grows in proportion to the grain size, so the effective outlet diameter shrinks considerably.

Now check the jamming condition again:

$$\frac{D}{d} = \frac{2.40}{0.60} = 4.0 < 6.$$

This hourglass does not flow at all. Once an arch forms, it does not collapse and the flow stops. The $327\,\mathrm{s}$ found above exists only on paper and would never be observed.

Larger grains are bad on two counts at once: the flow rate drops sharply, and the outlet moves close to the jamming limit. This is why hourglass makers sieve their grains to a narrow size range.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $D - kd = 1.56\,\mathrm{mm}$ | 0.2 |
| $T' = 327\,\mathrm{s}$ (300–350), factor 1.8 | 0.3 |
| $D/d = 4.0 < 6$, so it jams and does not flow | 0.2 |
:::

## Part D - Limits of precision (1.7 pt)

::: small
Syllabus: mathematics appendix (error propagation, Taylor expansion). The formula for the liquid-bridge force is outside the syllabus and is given in the text [5].
:::

::: q D.1 0.4pt
Error in $T$ caused by $\Delta D$.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

Since $T = M/Q_m \propto (D - kd)^{-5/2}$,

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac52\,\frac{\Delta D}{D - kd}.$$

For $\Delta D = 0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$,

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac52\times\frac{0.05}{1.98} = 6.3\,\% \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta T = \pm11\,\mathrm{s}.$$

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $\Delta T/T = (5/2)\Delta D/(D - kd)$ | 0.2 |
| $6.3\,\%$, $\Delta T = \pm11\,\mathrm{s}$ (10–12) | 0.2 |
:::

::: q D.2 0.4pt
Machining precision needed for 3 min $\pm$ 1 s.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

$$\frac{\Delta T}{T} = \frac{1}{180} = 5.56\times10^{-3} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta D = \frac25\times5.56\times10^{-3}\times1.98\,\mathrm{mm} = 4.4\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mm} = 4.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}.$$

Making a precise hourglass is a problem of machining the hole, not of clockmaking. The exponent $5/2$ amplifies any error in the hole.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $\Delta T/T = 1/180 = 5.56\times10^{-3}$ | 0.1 |
| $\Delta D = 4.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (4.2–4.6) | 0.3 |
:::

::: q D.3 0.4pt
Fraction of time in the final stage where the model fails.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

When the free surface is within $2D$ of the outlet, there is no layer of grains left to support an arch. This starts when the remaining height is $2D = 4.8\,\mathrm{mm}$. With a constant flow rate the fraction is

$$\frac{2D}{h_0} = \frac{4.8}{126} = 3.8\,\%.$$

The Beverloo law holds well for more than $96\,\%$ of the time, and the flow rate is disturbed only in the last few seconds. This is the stage at which the flow in a real hourglass visibly becomes irregular.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| Arch model fails for $h \lesssim 2D$ | 0.2 |
| $2D/h_0 = 3.8\,\%$ (3–5) | 0.2 |
:::

::: q D.4 0.5pt
Liquid-bridge force and the need for sealing.
:::

#### SOLUTION:

The weight of one grain is $W = \frac{\pi}{6}\rho_p g d^3$, so

$$\frac{F_{\mathrm{cap}}}{W} = \frac{\pi\gamma d}{\frac{\pi}{6}\rho_p g d^3} = \frac{6\gamma}{\rho_p g d^2} = \frac{6\times0.072}{2.50\times10^{3}\times9.81\times(3.0\times10^{-4})^2} = 1.96\times10^{2}.$$

One liquid bridge holds a grain with a force of nearly $200$ times its weight. The ratio equals $1$ at the critical diameter $d_c = \sqrt{6\gamma/\rho_p g} = 4.2\,\mathrm{mm}$. Grains of the size used in hourglasses therefore always clump together once there is moisture. An hourglass must be sealed with its sand dry, and this is the real reason the glass is closed completely.

#### MARKING SCHEME:

::: marking
| $F_{\mathrm{cap}}/W = 6\gamma/(\rho_p g d^2)$ | 0.2 |
| $= 1.96\times10^{2}$ ($1.8$–$2.1\times10^{2}$) | 0.2 |
| Moisture causes clumping $\Rightarrow$ must be sealed dry | 0.1 |
:::
