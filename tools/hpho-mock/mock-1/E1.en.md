Dip the corner of a sugar cube into coffee and the brown colour creeps upward. A paper towel soaking up water shows the same effect, as does part of the way a tree lifts water from its roots to its leaves. Narrow gaps pull the liquid in, the liquid meets viscous drag as it moves through them, and the balance of the two makes the wetted length grow as the square root of time. Washburn stated this law in 1921. Today you will check it.

The main question comes next. Paper is not a single smooth tube. It is a tangle of countless fibres. If a theory built on one tube still fits well, what does its "radius" actually measure? At the end you must deal on your own with a disturbance that nobody controls for you: evaporation.

### Background

When one end of a paper strip of width $b$ and thickness $t$ is dipped into a liquid, the wet front rises with time. If the paper is modelled as a bundle of thin tubes of radius $r$, the wetted length $L$ up to the front obeys

$$L^2 = K\,t_{\text{time}}, \qquad K = \frac{\gamma r\cos\theta}{2\mu},$$

where $\gamma$ is the surface tension, $\mu$ the viscosity and $\theta$ the contact angle. The value of $r$ obtained from this equation is called the *dynamic effective pore radius* $r_d$.

The porosity $\varphi$ of the paper is the fraction of its volume that is empty space. If the liquid fills all the voids in the wet part, the mass of liquid absorbed is $m = \varphi\rho\,b\,t\,L$.

With evaporation the front stops at a finite length. If each of the two wet faces loses a mass $E$ per unit area per unit time, then when the front stops

$$L_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{\varphi\rho\,t\,K}{4E}}.$$

## Part A - Checking the imbibition law (3.0 pt)

::: q A.1 0.8pt
Dip the lower end of a strip $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep into a dish of distilled water and measure the length $L$ up to the wet front as a function of time $t_{\text{time}}$. Take at least 8 data points spread evenly from $L = 2\,\mathrm{cm}$ to $L = 15\,\mathrm{cm}$. Record the measured values and their uncertainties in the table on the answer sheet.
:::

::: q A.2 0.9pt
Plot $\log L$ against $\log t_{\text{time}}$ on the graph paper of the answer sheet, and find the slope $n$ of the line with its uncertainty. Compare it with the value predicted by Washburn's law.
:::

::: q A.3 0.8pt
Taking $n = 1/2$, plot $L^2$ against $t_{\text{time}}$ and find $K$ with its uncertainty.
:::

::: q A.4 0.5pt
Taking $\theta = 0$, find the dynamic effective pore radius $r_d$ with its uncertainty.
:::

## Part B - What is paper made of? (2.0 pt)

::: q B.1 0.7pt
Measure the mass and the thickness $t$ of a dry strip. The thickness can be measured more precisely by stacking several strips. Then take a strip out of the water at the moment its wetted length reaches $L \approx 12\,\mathrm{cm}$, weigh it immediately and find the mass $m$ of liquid absorbed.
:::

::: q B.2 0.8pt
From $m = \varphi\rho_w\,b\,t\,L$, find the porosity $\varphi$ with its uncertainty.
:::

::: q B.3 0.5pt
Estimate from $\varphi$ the size of the gaps between fibres and compare it with $r_d$. If the two differ greatly, give one or two possible reasons.
:::

## Part C - Changing the surface tension (2.5 pt)

::: q C.1 1.0pt
Repeat A.1–A.3 with the surfactant solution and find $K_s$ with its uncertainty. Six or more data points are enough.
:::

::: q C.2 0.9pt
According to Washburn's law, $K \propto \gamma/\mu$ for the same paper. Use this to find the surface tension $\gamma_s$ of the surfactant solution with its uncertainty.
:::

::: q C.3 0.6pt
C.2 assumes that the two liquids have the same contact angle. If this assumption fails, will $\gamma_s$ come out larger or smaller than its true value? Justify your answer with an equation.
:::

## Part D - Evaporation: design your own measurement (2.5 pt)

In this part you decide the measurement procedure yourself. State on the answer sheet what you measured, how, and why. Numerical results without a written procedure receive no marks.

::: q D.1 1.0pt
Design and carry out a procedure to measure the saturation length $L_{\max}$ at which a front wetted by distilled water stops rising. State explicitly the criterion you used to decide that the front "no longer rises". Give the result and its uncertainty.
:::

::: q D.2 0.9pt
Using the equation given in the Background and your results from Parts A and B, find the evaporation rate $E$ with its uncertainty.
:::

::: q D.3 0.6pt
Determine $E$ once more by a method independent of the one above, and compare the two values. If they differ, point out at least one cause.
:::
