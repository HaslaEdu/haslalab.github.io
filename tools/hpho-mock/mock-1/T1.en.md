title: Why does an hourglass run steadily?

A water clock slows down as it empties. As the remaining water gets lighter, the pressure that drives it out of the hole drops. Ancient water clocks therefore had their scale marks packed closely at the top and spread out towards the bottom.

An hourglass does not behave like this. Fill the same volume through a hole of the same size, and the sand falls at the same rate from start to finish. It makes no difference whether half of it is left or a tenth. A granular pile has one property that sets it apart from a liquid, and this problem is about that property.

The usual explanation is that "in a granular pile the pressure at the bottom stops growing with depth". This is only half right. In this problem you first check with numbers how far that half holds. You then look for the real cause of the steady flow just above the outlet.

### Model

- A vertical cylinder of radius $R$ is filled to height $h_0$ with uniform spherical grains of diameter $d$. A circular outlet of diameter $D$ is cut in the centre of the base.
- The bulk density of the pile is $\rho_b = \varphi\rho_p$. The packing fraction $\varphi$ does not depend on depth.
- The depth $z$ is measured downwards from the free surface of the pile. The vertical stress $\sigma_v(z)$ inside the pile and the horizontal stress $\sigma_h$ on the wall are related by $\sigma_h = K\sigma_v$. $K$ is called the Janssen coefficient and is taken as constant. The wall pushes back on the pile with an upward friction stress $\tau_w = \mu_w\sigma_h$.
- Ignore the air between the grains. The grains are dry and do not stick to each other (except in D.4).
- In the water clock the water is inviscid and incompressible, and the hole area $a$ is much smaller than the cross-section $A$ of the cylinder.

## Part A - Why a water clock slows down (1.2 pt)

By Torricelli's theorem, water leaves a small hole at depth $h$ below the surface with speed $v = \sqrt{2gh}$.

::: q A.1 0.5pt
A cylindrical water clock of cross-section $A$ has a hole of area $a$ in its base. Write down the differential equation for the water height $h(t)$. Find the time $T_w$ for the clock to empty completely from the initial height $h_0$, in terms of $A$, $a$, $h_0$ and $g$.
:::

::: q A.2 0.3pt
Find the value of $T_w$ for a water clock with the $R$, $D$ and $h_0$ of Table 1.
:::

::: q A.3 0.4pt
Find the ratio of the time taken to drain the first half of the water to the time taken to drain the second half. From this result, state in one line the condition the flow rate must satisfy for the clock to keep steady time.
:::

## Part B - Janssen stress saturation (3.3 pt)

Now fill the same cylinder with grains instead of water. **In Part B the outlet is closed with a plug and the grains are at rest.** The plug area is $1\,\%$ of the base area and is neglected. The first way a granular pile differs from a liquid is that it also transmits stress sideways and passes part of it to the wall.

::: q B.1 1.1pt
Write the balance of vertical forces on the thin layer between depths $z$ and $z+dz$, and derive the differential equation for $\sigma_v(z)$. Solve it with $\sigma_v(0)=0$ at the free surface. Write the result in the form

$$\sigma_v(z) = \rho_b g\lambda\left(1 - e^{-z/\lambda}\right)$$

with a characteristic length $\lambda$, and express $\lambda$ in terms of $R$, $\mu_w$ and $K$.
:::

::: q B.2 0.4pt
Find the value of $\lambda$ and the vertical stress $\sigma_v(h_0)$ at the base when the cylinder is filled to $h_0$. What fraction is this of the hydrostatic pressure at the same depth?
:::

::: q B.3 0.5pt
Find the force actually carried by the base and the total weight of the grains, and give their ratio as a percentage. What carries the rest?
:::

::: q B.4 0.6pt
Express in terms of $\lambda$ the condition for the vertical stress at the base to be at least $95\,\%$ of its saturated value, and find the corresponding height. As you will see later, the mass flow rate of the grains is constant in time. Take this as given and find the fraction of the total discharge time during which the condition is not met. Can Janssen saturation alone explain why an hourglass keeps steady time?
:::

::: q B.5 0.7pt
The cylinder is clamped to a stand. Only its base plate, which does not touch the cylinder wall, rests on a balance. Grains are poured in a little at a time. When the fill height is $h$, find the apparent mass $M_{\mathrm{app}}(h)$ shown by the balance. What happens to $M_{\mathrm{app}}$ as $h$ keeps increasing? Find the limiting value, and check that the value at $h_0$ of Table 1 agrees with your result in B.3.
:::

## Part C - The free-fall arch and the Beverloo law (3.8 pt)

Just above the outlet the grains brace against each other and form a dome-shaped arch. All the weight above the arch is passed sideways through it to the wall, and the grains below the arch carry no stress at all. The grains below the arch therefore start from rest and fall freely. Take the height of the arch to be proportional to the outlet diameter.

::: q C.1 1.0pt
Show that in this model the speed of the grains passing through the outlet scales as $v \propto \sqrt{gD}$. Then use dimensional analysis to derive that the mass flow rate scales as

$$Q_m \propto \rho_b\sqrt{g}\,D^{5/2}.$$
:::

::: q C.2 0.6pt
In reality no grains pass through a ring of width $\sim d/2$ at the edge of the outlet. Writing this as an effective outlet diameter $D - kd$ gives the Beverloo law

$$Q_m = C\rho_b\sqrt{g}\,(D-kd)^{5/2}.$$

The empty-ring argument alone gives $k \approx 1$. It fixes only the order of $k$; this problem uses the measured value $k = 1.4$ of Table 1. Explain what this formula says about the steady running of an hourglass.
:::

::: q C.3 0.6pt
Find the value of $Q_m$, the total mass $M$ of grains in the cylinder, and the time $T$ for this hourglass to run out.
:::

::: q C.4 0.4pt
From the result of C.3, find the mean speed of the grains at the outlet. Trace it back as free fall to estimate the height of the arch. Is this value reasonable compared with $D$?
:::

::: q C.5 0.5pt
If the outlet is too narrow, the arch does not collapse and the flow stops altogether. Experiments show that the outlet does not jam if $D/d \gtrsim 6$ [4]. Find the smallest outlet diameter this condition allows, and state as a percentage how much margin the value in Table 1 has above it.
:::

::: q C.6 0.7pt
The same hourglass is filled with grains of twice the diameter ($d = 0.60\,\mathrm{mm}$). Find how the discharge time changes. Then check the jamming condition of C.5 again, and decide whether this hourglass would actually work.
:::

## Part D - Limits of precision (1.7 pt)

::: q D.1 0.4pt
Using the Beverloo law, write an expression for how much a relative error in the outlet diameter is amplified in the relative error of the discharge time. For a machining tolerance $\Delta D = \pm 0.05\,\mathrm{mm}$, find the relative and the absolute error of $T$.
:::

::: q D.2 0.4pt
How precisely must the outlet be machined to sell this hourglass as "3 min $\pm$ 1 s"?
:::

::: q D.3 0.4pt
Once the free surface comes close to the outlet and the remaining height falls below $2D$, the arch model no longer holds. Find the fraction of the total discharge time spent in this final stage.
:::

::: q D.4 0.5pt
In humid air, liquid bridges of water form between grains, and the force pulling two grains together is $F_{\mathrm{cap}} \approx \pi\gamma d$. Express the ratio of this force to the weight of one grain in terms of $\gamma$, $\rho_p$, $g$ and $d$, and find its value. Explain why an hourglass must be sealed.
:::
