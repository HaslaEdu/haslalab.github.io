$v_2^2 - 3v_2 + 2 = 0$

Collision between $D$ and $B$ perfectly inelastic

(iii)By conservation of momentum:

$\bullet$  before the collision (in magnitude) → $Q_i = M\,v_{2-} + M_Dv_{3-} = 6\times1 + 2\times3 = 12$

b)  Collision between $A$ and $C$ perfectly inelastic

(i)This situation is the same as in item (i) of part (a). Thus $v_1$, the velocity of $M = \left(M_A + M_B + \mathit{Bar} + M_C\right) = 6\ \mathrm{kg}$, is equal to $2\ \mathrm{m/s}$.

Collision between $C$ and $D$ also perfectly inelastic

(ii)By conservation of momentum:

$\bullet$  before the collision (in magnitude) → $Q_i = M\,v_1 = 6\times2$

5. The efficiency of a Carnot engine is given by $\eta_C = 1 - \dfrac{T_f}{T_q} = 1 - \dfrac{300}{600}$, that is, $\eta_C = 0.5$.

Since the engine in the problem has an efficiency 20% lower, then $\eta = 0.4$.

a) According to the statement, the heat flow coming from the hot source, which enters the engine, is numerically equal to that of a bar subjected to a temperature difference of 500 K, that is:

$\dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t} = k\,A\dfrac{\Delta T}{l}$

$\Rightarrow$

$\dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t} = 50\times2\times\dfrac{500}{0.1}$

$\Rightarrow$

$\dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t} = 500\ \mathrm{kW}$

The efficiency of an engine is defined as $\eta = \dfrac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta Q_q}$, where $\Delta\tau$ is the work done.

Thus, $\dfrac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta t} = \eta\dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t}$, that is, the power of the engine is:

$P = \dfrac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta t} = 200\ \mathrm{kW}$

b) In a heat engine, the heat rejected to the cold source is:

$\Delta Q_f = \Delta Q_q - \Delta\tau$

$\Rightarrow$

$\dfrac{\Delta Q_f}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t} - \dfrac{\Delta\tau}{\Delta t} = \left(1 - \eta\right)\dfrac{\Delta Q_q}{\Delta t}$

Hence, $\dfrac{\Delta Q_f}{\Delta t} = 300\ \mathrm{kJ/s}$

The amount of heat rejected in 10 minutes (= 600 s) will be:

$\Delta Q_f = 300\times10^{3}\times600$

$\Rightarrow$

$\Delta Q_f = 1.8\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{J}$

The amount of heat needed to raise a mass m of ice from $T_0 = -20\,{}^{o}\mathrm{C}$ to $0\,{}^{o}\mathrm{C}$ and then melt it is:

$\Delta Q_f = m\,c_g\,(T - T_o) + m\,L\,,$

where $c_g = 2100\ \mathrm{J/(kg\,{}^{0}C)}$ is the specific heat of ice and $L = 330\ \mathrm{kJ/kg}$ is the latent heat of fusion of ice. Thus,

$1.8\times10^{8} = m\left(2100\times20 + 330\times10^{3}\right)$

This gives $m = 483.9\ \mathrm{kg}$

Note: Owing to an error by the exam committee, the values of the specific heat cg and of the latent heat of fusion L of ice were not provided. Therefore, full marks were awarded to students who solved the question correctly but did not present the final numerical result.

a) If the process A → B is isobaric, the pressure in state B is equal to that of state A, that is $P_B = P_A = 32\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{N/m^2}$. The volume of the gas in B, according to the statement, is $V_B = 2\ \mathrm{m^3}$.

Since the process B → C is adiabatic, then $P_BV_B^{\gamma} = P_CV_C^{\gamma} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_C = \left(\dfrac{P_B}{P_C}\right)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}V_B\,.$

Since $\gamma = \dfrac{c_p}{c_v}$, then $\gamma = \dfrac{5}{3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_C = \left(32\right)^{3/5}V_B \Rightarrow \quad V_C = 16\ \mathrm{m^3}$

The PV diagram will then be:

Fig. 03

b) the work in the expansion A → C will be:

$\Delta\tau = \Delta\tau_{AB} + \Delta\tau_{BC}$

$\bullet$The work in the isobaric process A → B is

$\Delta\tau_{AB} = P_A\left(V_B - V_A\right)$

$\Delta\tau_{AB} = 32\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$

$\bullet$In the adiabatic process B → C the work is:

$\Delta\tau_{BC} = \dfrac{1}{1-\gamma}\left(P_CV_C - P_BV_B\right)$

$\Delta\tau_{BC} = \dfrac{1}{1-5/3}\left(1\times16 - 32\times2\right)\times10^{5} = 72\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$

$\bullet$The total work will therefore be:

$\Delta\tau = 104\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$

c) The total change in internal energy will be $\Delta U_{AC} = \Delta U_{AB} + \Delta U_{BC}$

From the first law of thermodynamics, we have:

$\Delta U = \Delta Q - \Delta\tau$

(1)

$\bullet$Since the process A → B is isobaric, then:

$\Delta U_{AB} = n\,C_p\,\Delta T - P\Delta V$

Using the ideal-gas equation, $PV = nRT$, and the fact that the pressure remains constant, we have: $\Delta T = \dfrac{P\Delta V}{nR}$, so that:

$\Delta U_{AB} = \left(\dfrac{C_p}{R} - 1\right)P\Delta V = \dfrac{C_v}{R}P\Delta V$, since $C_p = C_v + R$

But $P\Delta V = P_A\left(V_B - V_A\right) = \tau_{AB}$

Since $C_v = \dfrac{3}{2}R$, then

$\Delta U_{AB} = \dfrac{3}{2}\tau_{AB} = 48\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$

$\bullet$The process B → C is adiabatic, so that $\Delta Q = 0$. Thus, from (1) we must have:

$\Delta U_{BC} = -\Delta\tau_{BC} = -72\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$

$\bullet$The change in internal energy over the whole process will be:

$\Delta U_{AC} = -24\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{J}$
