Problem 1.1 The sinking rod

While the rod is being lowered, the following forces act on it:

A) The force of gravity, applied at the centre of mass of the rod (the rod is uniform, so at its middle) and directed vertically downward, equal to

$F_0=mg=\rho_0 Sl\,g$(1)

$S$ is the cross-sectional area of the rod.

B) The buoyant (Archimedes) force, applied at the centre of mass of the displaced liquid (the middle of the submerged part of the rod, of length $z$) and directed vertically upward, equal to

$F_A=\rho Sz\,g\,.$(2)

C) The required tension $F$ of the thread is applied at the upper end of the rod and directed vertically upward.

D) If the rod touches the bottom, the normal reaction force of the bottom $\vec N$, directed vertically upward, acts at the point of contact (the lower end of the rod).

Since the rod is lowered slowly, at every moment the rod can be taken to be in equilibrium, i.e. the sum of the forces acting on the rod and the sum of their torques are zero.

Let us now consider the different stages of lowering the rod.

Problem 1.2 A bug on a cord

Let us picture the motion of the bug as motion along a circle whose radius grows with time.

The circumference of the circle (equal to the length of the cord) grows with time as

$L=l_0+Vt\,.$(1)

Hence its radius changes as

$R(t)=\dfrac L{2\pi}=\dfrac{l_0+Vt}{2\pi}\,.$(2)

In a short time interval $dt$ the bug crawls the distance $vdt$, i.e. it turns through the angle

$d\varphi=\dfrac{2\pi vdt}{l_0+Vt}$(3)

Integrating this expression, we obtain the angle through which the bug has turned as a function of time

$\varphi(t)=\displaystyle\int_0^t\dfrac{2\pi vdt}{l_0+Vt}=2\pi v\int_0^t\dfrac{dt}{l_0+Vt}=2\pi\dfrac vV\ln\dfrac{l_0+Vt}{l_0}\,.$(4)

When the bug has crawled half of the cord, its angle of turn is $\varphi=\pi$, and when it reaches the end of the cord, $\varphi=2\pi$.

The problem can also be solved without the artificial, though illustrative, "rolling" of the cord into a circle. One can proceed more formally.

Option 1.

Let the position of the bug be given by its coordinate $x$, its distance from the fixed wall. This coordinate then satisfies the equation:

$\dfrac{dx}{dt}=V\dfrac x{l_0+Vt}+v$(5)

It is not hard to solve this equation and then to set the coordinate of the bug equal to the changing length of the cord.

Option 2. Let $\xi=\dfrac x{l_0+Vt}$ be the fraction of the length of the cord that the bug has crawled. Then this quantity satisfies the equation

$\dfrac{d\xi}{dt}=\dfrac v{l_0+Vt}$(6)

This equation follows both from simple logical reasoning and as a consequence of equation (5). Note that it is fully equivalent to equation (3).

The solution of this equation is

$\xi=\dfrac vV\ln\dfrac{l_0+Vt}{l_0}\,.$(3)

From which the required times follow:

To the middle of the cord

$\tau_{0.5}=\dfrac{l_0}V\exp\left(\dfrac12\dfrac Vv\right)\approx9.2\cdot10^{17}\ \text{years}$

To the end of the cord

$\tau_{0.5}=\dfrac{l_0}V\exp\left(\dfrac Vv\right)\approx8.5\cdot10^{35}\ \text{years}.$

Problem 1.3. A modernized Young's scheme

Let the central slit $S_0$ produce at the point $A$ of the screen, at the distance $x$ from the axis of symmetry, an oscillation described by the function

$E_0=A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0)$        (1)

The quantity $E_0$ can be regarded as the electric field strength of the light wave. Then the oscillations produced by the side slits at the same point are described by the functions

$E_{1,2}=A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0\pm\Delta\varphi)\,.$(2)

The quantity $\Delta\varphi$ is the additional phase shift due to the displacement of the slits by the distance $d$. As is well known, this phase shift is proportional to the coordinate $x$ of the point $A$ on the screen

$\Delta\varphi=ax\,,$(3)

where $a$ is a constant that depends on the wavelength, the distance between the slits and the distance between the plate and the screen. The exact expression for this constant is not needed to solve the problem.

1.3.1 With only two slits, the resulting oscillation is described by the function

$E=A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0-\Delta\varphi)+A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0+\Delta\varphi)=2A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0)\cdot\cos(\Delta\varphi)$(4)

The intensity of light is proportional to the time average of the square of the field strength. With only two slits this intensity is

$I_{(2)}=\left\langle\left(2A\cos(\omega t-\varphi_0)\cdot\cos(\Delta\varphi)\right)^2\right\rangle=2A^2\cos^2(\Delta\varphi)=4I_{(1)}\cos^2(\Delta\varphi)$(5)

where $I_{(1)}$ is the intensity of light produced by one source.

The same result can be obtained by adding the oscillations with the method of vector (phasor) diagrams. The diagram of the oscillations is shown in the figure; it shows that the amplitude of the resulting oscillation is

$A_{(2)}=2A\cos\Delta\varphi$(6)

Then the intensity of light, proportional to the square of the amplitude, is

$I_{(2)}=4I_{(1)}\cos^2\Delta\varphi=4I_{(1)}\cos^2ax\,.$(7)

The constant $a$ is easily found from the graph given. It is convenient to write it as

Problem 2. Physics of popcorn

Part 1. Critical bursting pressure

1.1 Since the expansion of the gases after the shell bursts is very fast, this process can be taken to be adiabatic. We use the equation of the adiabatic process

$P_{cr}V_0^\gamma=P_0V_1^\gamma$(1)

The expansion goes from the initial volume of the kernel $V_0$ (at a pressure equal to the critical bursting pressure $P_{cr}$) to the volume of the flake formed $V_1$ (at a pressure equal to atmospheric). Since the volumes of the particles are proportional to the cube of their radii, the required critical pressure is given by

$P_{cr}=P_0\left(\dfrac{V_1}{V_0}\right)^\gamma=P_0\left(\dfrac{r_1}{r_0}\right)^{3\gamma}\approx19\,{\rm atm}=19\cdot10^5\,{\rm Pa}\,.$(2)

1.2 The change of the mass of the kernel (i.e. the mass of the evaporated moisture) can be neglected. Then the ratio of the volumes equals the inverse ratio of the densities, so

$P_{cr}=P_0\left(\dfrac{V_1}{V_0}\right)^\gamma=P_0\left(\dfrac{\rho_0}{\rho_1}\right)^\gamma\approx16\,{\rm atm}=16\cdot10^5\,{\rm Pa}\,.$(3)

1.3 To relate the maximum pressure inside the kernel to the maximum mechanical stress the shell can withstand, we can use the Laplace formula for the excess pressure. The analogue of the surface tension coefficient (the force per unit length) is the product of the maximum strength and the thickness of the shell, $\sigma_ch$. By the Laplace formula, the pressure difference inside and outside the shell is

$\Delta P=P_{cr}-P_0=\dfrac{2\sigma_ch}{r_0}\,.$(4)

From this formula the critical pressure is estimated as

$P_{cr}=\dfrac{2\sigma_ch}{r_0}+P_0\approx11\cdot10^5\,{\rm Pa}=11\,{\rm atm}\,.$(5)

1.4 Of the three results obtained, the last one (5) is preferable, since the previous calculations use poorly defined parameters of the kernels and flakes: the mean radii and densities of the kernels before and after heating.

1.5 The condition that the ball does not leave the inner surface of the sphere during its motion can be stated as follows: the normal reaction force $\vec N$ of the sphere is always larger than zero. For this condition to hold, the projection of the Coulomb force on the radial direction must be larger than or equal to the projection of gravity on the same direction, i.e.

$F\cos\dfrac\theta2>mg\cos\theta\ \Rightarrow\ mg\dfrac f{\cos\dfrac\theta2}\ge mg\cos\theta\ \Rightarrow\ f\ge\cos\theta\cos\dfrac\theta2\,.$(9)

The maximum value of the product of the cosines is 1, so the required condition finally reads

$f\ge1.$(10)

In other words, if the ball does not leave the surface at the top point, it does not leave it at any other point. Note also that as the ball moves, the reaction force of the surface of the sphere can only grow, so the ball certainly does not leave the sphere.

Part 2. Motion of the ball for $f=1$.

2.1 From the dependence of the potential energy on the deflection angle at $f=1$ it follows that the ball starts to slide over the surface of the sphere, passes the equilibrium position by inertia and stops at the point where the change of its potential energy becomes zero. The value of the angle $\theta_{\max}$ at the extreme position can be found as the solution of the equation

$\Delta U=2mgR\left(\cos^2\dfrac\theta2+\dfrac f{\cos\dfrac\theta2}-1-f\right)=0\,.$(11)

Denote $\cos\dfrac{\theta_{\max}}2=z$; this quantity then satisfies the equation:

$z^2+\dfrac fz-1-f=0\ \Rightarrow\ z^3-2z+1=0\,.$(12)

This equation has one obvious root $z_1=1$, corresponding to the top position of the ball (the initial position). Knowing this root lets us transform equation (12) to the form:

$z^3-2z+1=(z^3-z)-(z-1)=z(z^2-1)-(z-1)=(z-1)(z(z+1)-1)=0\,.$(13)

So besides the root $z_1=1$ this equation has two more roots (the roots of a quadratic equation):

$z^2+z-1=0\ \Rightarrow\ z_{2,3}=\dfrac{-1\pm\sqrt{1+4}}2\,.$(14)

One of these roots exceeds 1 in magnitude, so the cosine cannot be equal to it for real values of the angle. So we need the root $z_2=\dfrac{\sqrt{1+4}-1}2\approx0.618$, which corresponds to the maximum deflection angle

$\theta_{\max}\approx104^\circ\,.$(15)

2.2 In equilibrium the sum of the torques acting on the ball is zero, so

$mgR\sin\theta=mgR\dfrac{f\sin\dfrac\theta2}{\cos^2\dfrac\theta2}\,.$(16)

From this equation we find

$2\sin\dfrac\theta2\cos\dfrac\theta2=\dfrac{\sin\dfrac\theta2}{\cos^2\dfrac\theta2}\ \Rightarrow\ \cos^3\dfrac\theta2=\dfrac12\ \Rightarrow\ \bar\theta=2\arccos\dfrac1{\sqrt[3]2}\approx75^\circ\,.$(17)

As shown above, at this point the potential energy of the ball is minimal, so this equilibrium position is stable. Remark: the same result can be obtained by setting the derivative of the potential energy equal to zero.

2.3 To describe the motion of the ball along a horizontal circle it is convenient to go over to a rotating frame of reference in which the ball is at rest. In this frame the centrifugal force of inertia $\vec F_C$ acting on the ball must be added. This force depends on the speed of the rotation, so it can take any positive value. The torque of this force has the same direction as the torque of gravity. Therefore equilibrium is possible only in the region where the magnitude of the torque of the Coulomb force is larger than the magnitude of the torque of gravity, i.e. for

$\bar\theta<\theta<\dfrac\pi2\,.$(18)

Part 3. Motion of the ball for $f=2$

The value of the parameter $f=2$ is critical (a bifurcation point). As shown above (see formulas (7)–(8)), at this value the sum of the restoring torques vanishes in the linear approximation. However, in this case too the top point is a point of stable equilibrium, since the potential curve has a minimum there. The expansion of the torques must therefore be carried to the next order of smallness in the deflection angle $\theta$. Since the torques are described by odd functions, the next order is the third, i.e. the total torque acting on the ball can be written as

$M=M_g-M_e=mgR\sin\theta-2mgR\dfrac{\sin\dfrac\theta2}{\cos^2\dfrac\theta2}=-mgR\left(2\dfrac{\sin\dfrac\theta2}{\cos^2\dfrac\theta2}-\sin\theta\right)=-CmgR\cdot\theta^3\,,$(18)

where $C$ is a dimensionless coefficient.

Then the equation of motion of the ball near the top point is:

$mR^2\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-CmgR\theta^3\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-C\dfrac gR\theta^3$(19)
Theoretical round. Solutions.

This is a nonlinear equation describing anharmonic oscillations. Since the problem only asks for the form of the dependence of the period of oscillation on their amplitude, there is no need to look for the exact solution of equation (19). For this we bring the equation to a "dimensionless" form. We introduce the variable

$\eta=\dfrac\theta{\theta_0}\,,$(20)

the ratio of the deflection angle to its amplitude (maximum) value. We also introduce our own unit of time $t_0$, whose value we shall determine later. Time measured in units of $t_0$ is related to time in seconds by

$\tau=\dfrac t{t_0}\,.$(21)

We now rewrite equation (19) in the units we introduced ($\theta=\eta\theta_0,\ t=\tau t_0$):

$\dfrac{\theta_0}{t_0^2}\dfrac{d^2\eta}{d\tau^2}=-C\dfrac gR\theta_0^3\cdot\eta^3\,.$(22)

Now we choose the unit of time so that all the coefficients in this equation cancel, i.e.

$\dfrac{\theta_0}{t_0^2}=C\dfrac gR\theta_0^3\ \Rightarrow\ t_0=\dfrac1{\theta_0}\sqrt{C\dfrac Rg}\,.$(23)

With this unit of time equation (22)

$\dfrac{d^2\eta}{dt^2}=-\eta^3\,.$(24)

contains no parameters; moreover, the variable $\eta$ varies between -1 and +1. Therefore the period of oscillation in units of $t_0$ is some number (independent of anything). Going back to the usual units of time, we conclude that the period of oscillation is proportional to $t_0$, so the formula for the period can be written as

$T=\dfrac{C_0}{\theta_0}\sqrt{\dfrac Rg}\,.$(25)

Part 4. Motion of the ball for $f=3$.

4.1 For $f>2$ the oscillations of the ball near the top point are harmonic, since the total torque acting on the ball is proportional to the deflection angle at small angles. We use formulas (7)–(8) and write the equation of motion:

$mR^2\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-mgR\left(\dfrac f2-1\right)\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}=-\dfrac g{2R}\theta\,,$(26)

which is the equation of harmonic oscillations with the period

$T=2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{2R}g}\,.$(27)

4.2 We write explicitly the equilibrium condition of the ball in the rotating non-inertial frame of reference. The torque of the centrifugal force of inertia in this frame is

$M_C=\dfrac{mv_1^2}{R\sin\theta}R\cos\theta\approx\dfrac{mv_1^2}\theta\,.$(28)

Then the condition that the torques are equal takes the form

$|M_e|-M_g=M_C\ \Rightarrow\ \dfrac32mgR\theta-mgR\cdot\theta=\dfrac{mv_1^2}\theta\,.$(29)
